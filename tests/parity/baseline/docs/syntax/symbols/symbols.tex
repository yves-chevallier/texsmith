\title{Smart symbols and quotes}
\author{}\date{}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
Two substitutions run while TMark scans a run of text: the smart symbols of
\tscodeinline{pymdownx.smartsymbols}, and TeXSmith's own double-quote pattern. Both produce
ordinary text in the document model --- there is nothing to reference and nothing
to configure per occurrence --- and both are class E: every MkDocs site already
does the same.
\section{Smart symbols}
\begin{tscode}[lang=md, engine=pygments]
\begin{Verbatim}[commandchars=\\\{\},breaklines, breakanywhere, commandchars=\\\{\}]
Copyright (c) 2025, tea (tm), reg (r), c/o Ada: 1/2 a cup, +/\PYZhy{} 3, 4 =/= 5, a \PYZhy{}\PYZhy{}\PYZgt{} b.
\end{Verbatim}
\end{tscode}
renders as
\begin{displayquote}
Copyright \tsemoji{©} 2025, tea \tsemoji{™}, reg \tsemoji{®}, \tsscript{symbols}{℅ }Ada: ½ a cup, \(\pm\) 3, 4 \tsscript{mathematics}{\(\neq\) }5, a \tsscript{symbols}{\(\rightarrow\) }b.
\end{displayquote}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X}
\toprule
\textbf{Group} & \textbf{Spellings} & \textbf{Result} \\
\midrule
Marks & \tscodeinline{(c)} \tscodeinline{(tm)} \tscodeinline{(r)} & \tsemoji{©} \tsemoji{™} \tsemoji{®} \\
Care of & \tscodeinline{c/o} & \tsscript{symbols}{℅} \\
Operators & \tscodeinline{+/-\allowbreak{}} \tscodeinline{=/=} & \(\pm\) \tsscript{mathematics}{\(\neq\)} \\
Arrows & \tscodeinline{-\allowbreak{}-\allowbreak{}>} \tscodeinline{<-\allowbreak{}-\allowbreak{}} \tscodeinline{<-\allowbreak{}-\allowbreak{}>} & \tsscript{symbols}{\(\rightarrow\) \(\leftarrow\) }\tsemoji{↔} \\
Fractions & \tscodeinline{1/2} \tscodeinline{1/4} \tscodeinline{3/4} \tscodeinline{1/3} \tscodeinline{2/3} \tscodeinline{1/5} ... \tscodeinline{7/8} & ½ ¼ ¾ \tsscript{symbols}{⅓ ⅔ ⅕ }... \tsscript{symbols}{⅞} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
The extension's own boundaries apply: an arrow is not part of a longer run of
dashes (\tscodeinline{-\allowbreak{}-\allowbreak{}-\allowbreak{}>} stays as typed), a fraction is not part of a longer number
(\tscodeinline{11/2} and \tscodeinline{1/25} stay as typed), and \tscodeinline{c/o} must be a whole word.
\begin{tscallout}[kind=note, title={What is \emph{not} substituted}]
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ordinal numbers.} \tscodeinline{1st} would be a superscript rather than a
character, so it is left alone.
\item \textbf{\tscodeinline{-\allowbreak{}-\allowbreak{}}, \tscodeinline{-\allowbreak{}-\allowbreak{}-\allowbreak{}} and \tscodeinline{...}.} Both paged backends typeset the ASCII
spelling as the dash and the ellipsis on their own; converting them would
gain nothing and lose the round-trip.
\item \textbf{Anything in code.} No substitution fires inside a code span or a
fenced block.
\end{itemize}
\end{tscallout}
\section{Quotes}
A straight-quoted phrase becomes a proper quotation, typeset for the document's
language:
\begin{tscode}[lang=md, engine=pygments]
\begin{Verbatim}[commandchars=\\\{\},breaklines, breakanywhere, commandchars=\\\{\}]
He said \PYZdq{}straight quotes\PYZdq{}, \PY{l+s+sb}{`\PYZdq{}not in code\PYZdq{}`}, and \PYZbs{}\PYZdq{}not a quote\PYZdq{}.
\end{Verbatim}
\end{tscode}
In \LaTeX{} that is \tscodeinline{\textbackslash{}enquote\{straight quotes\}}, so French gets guillemets, German
gets low-high quotes and English gets curly quotes --- from the root \tscodeinline{lang:} key
or the nearest \tscodeinline{lang=} attribute, never from the source spelling.

Three rules bound it:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Double quotes only.} Single quotes are never paired: an apostrophe is not
a quote.
\item \textbf{One text run.} A pair whose quotes sit on either side of inline markup
stays literal, because the scan reads a run of text and not the whole
paragraph.
\item \textbf{\tscodeinline{\textbackslash{}"} escapes.} A backslash before the opening quote leaves the phrase
alone.
\end{itemize}
\begin{tscallout}[kind=tip, title={Why this matters for print}]
\end{tscallout}
\begin{tscode}[lang=text, engine=pygments]
\begin{Verbatim}[commandchars=\\\{\},breaklines, breakanywhere, commandchars=\\\{\}]
Straight quotes in a PDF are a typographic error, and no template can fix
them after the fact: by the time the text reaches LaTeX, the language is
the only thing that knows which glyphs to use. Writing `\PYZdq{}…\PYZdq{}` and letting
the substitution run is what puts `\PYZbs{}enquote\PYZob{}…\PYZcb{}` — and therefore the right
glyphs — in the output.
\end{Verbatim}
\end{tscode}
\section{Language-driven spacing}
Punctuation spacing is not a substitution and never a construct in the body:
the narrow no-break space before \tscodeinline{;}, \tscodeinline{:}, \tscodeinline{?} and \tscodeinline{!} in French, and the
no-break space between a number and its unit, are applied by the backend from
the document language. \tscodeinline{\&nbsp;} is accepted as an explicit override.

A passage in another language is a span:
\begin{tscode}[lang=md, engine=pygments]
\begin{Verbatim}[commandchars=\\\{\},breaklines, breakanywhere, commandchars=\\\{\}]
A French sentence with [a quotation in English]\PYZob{}lang=en\PYZcb{} inside it.
\end{Verbatim}
\end{tscode}
See also Emoji and icons for the other two colon-delimited
substitutions.
